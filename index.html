The Price of Europe — the crisis is over; the receipt says otherwise.

A data essay · 2019 – 2025

Take €100 of monthly spending from January 2019. Same items, same shops — watch the register.

Inflation is back near 2%. So why does the total never go back down?

Introduction

Mojtaba Alehosseini

Supervised by Annalisa Barla

Between 2019 and 2025 the cost of living in the European Union rose faster than at any point since the euro began. The crisis is reported as over. The prices it left behind are not. This is the story of one hundred euros, and where they went.

2021–22

The lead narrows

Prices accelerate through the energy shock and the median floor's lead shrinks by about a third. It never disappears — which is exactly the problem with medians.

2023–24

Pay answers

Governments raise floors, one after another, and the lead widens again — money arriving after the damage was done.

2025

The finish line

The median floor ends about 20 points above prices. But at the worst of 2022, seventeen of these twenty-one countries sat below where they started. Averages end races; people carry what happened along the way.

How this was made

All data are pulled directly from the Eurostat JSON API by the Python scripts in preprocessing/. Five tables: harmonised consumer prices (monthly, annual, indexed), minimum wages, and the house price index. A topojson of EU borders comes from the leakymirror fork of world-atlas. No proprietary sources.

The site is hand-written HTML, CSS custom properties, and ES modules. The only run-time dependencies are D3 v7, Scrollama, topojson-client, and flubber (used in the Choropleth → Bar morph for path interpolation) — all loaded as globals from a CDN. Animations use the project's own lightweight MotionManager module plus D3 transitions and CSS keyframes, with a static fallback wherever prefers-reduced-motion is set.

Methodological choices worth knowing

  • EU-27 aggregate — Choropleth uses Eurostat's official EU27_2020 annual HICP figures (1.4 % for 2019, 9.2 % for 2022, 2.6 % for 2024) rather than a naïve country-mean.
  • Small multiples, one scale — the six category panels share a single y-axis across Jan 2019 – Dec 2021, so the height of each line is directly comparable: energy and transport tower over the rest because they genuinely did, not because each panel was scaled to fit. Each panel's tag is its value at the end of that window; the peak month appears in the enlarged view.
  • Heatmap cells — one cell per category and per month, with no averaging: colour is that month's own year-on-year rate for the EU-27, 2021 – 2024, on the same green-to-red scale the map uses. Reading across a row shows how long a category stayed hot.
  • Real-wage formula — the diverging bar computes real change as (1 + nominal wage Δ) / (1 + cumulative HICP Δ) − 1, where nominal pay is the statutory minimum wage in national currency (Eurostat earn_mw_cur, currency = NAC) deflated by that country's own HICP. Six EU countries are excluded, for two different reasons: Austria, Denmark, Finland, Italy and Sweden set wages through collective bargaining rather than a statutory minimum; Cyprus introduced a statutory minimum on 1 January 2023, too late to measure against a 2019 baseline.
  • Why the minimum wage — it is the one wage every EU country publishes on the same basis and at the same dates, as a policy number rather than a survey estimate: a statutory floor, set by law, comparable across borders without adjusting for hours, sector or seniority. Average earnings would answer a different question — what people actually earn — and would arrive later, less comparably, with composition effects mixed into the change.
  • Box-plot outliers — Tukey's 1.5 × IQR rule, applied per year across the 27 country values. Outliers shown as larger orange dots; the faint dot strip beneath each box is every country's actual value with deterministic horizontal jitter.
  • The receipt & the basket — the hero and kitchen-table basket use illustrative renter-household weights (30/22/15/14/10/9 over housing, groceries, services, transport, energy, eating out), anchored to the EU Household Budget Survey's structure but excluding imputed rents, which never appear on a cash receipt. Each line grows by its Eurostat HICP category index; lines are rounded to the cent, then summed.
  • Rate vs level — "inflation returned to 2%" and "prices returned to normal" are different claims. Chapter 5 shows both: the monthly rate (speed) and the 2019-based index (height). The essay's argument lives in the difference.
  • House prices — the house price index tracks buying, not renting; HICP's housing component covers rents, utilities and maintenance. Chapter 7 keeps the two claims separate on purpose.

The design decisions behind every chart — what each answers, what was rejected, and why — are in docs/design_decisions.md. Source code is at github.com/mojtaba-alehoseini/price-of-europe.
