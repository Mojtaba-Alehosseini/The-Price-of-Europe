The price of everything, suddenly, all at once.

Between 2019 and 2025 Europe absorbed three overlapping shocks — a pandemic, an energy war, and a wage chase — and consumer prices climbed faster than at any point in two generations. Here is how that crisis unfolded, country by country, category by category.

The European Central Bank's inflation target is two percent. For nearly a decade after the eurozone crisis, that target held: prices drifted, wages crept, debates about deflation outnumbered debates about the cost of bread. Then 2021 arrived, and the line that had been almost flat for ten years bent sharply upward.

This piece does not try to argue that prices rose — anyone who bought electricity, pasta, or a Berlin apartment in 2022 knows that already. It tries to show which prices rose, where the rises hit hardest, and what happened to the wages meant to keep up. Each chart is a small instrument: rotate it, scrub the slider, hover over a country. The numbers are from Eurostat, the same source the ECB watches.

Act I · 2015 – 2019

The Calm

Five quiet years when the European Central Bank worried, mostly, about prices being too low. The geography of inflation looked nearly uniform — Estonia, Italy, Portugal all roughly the same shade of nothing-to-see.

2019 · Before

A continent on cruise control

In 2019 the European Union's annual inflation rate was 1.4 % — comfortably below the ECB's target. The map shows almost every country in the muted greens of the sub-target band. The Baltic states ran slightly warmer, southern Europe slightly cooler, but the spread between them was a couple of percentage points at most.

2021 · Heating

The first shade darker

As supply chains unkinked after the pandemic, prices began catching up. By December 2021 the eurozone hit 5.0 %. The map turns amber across the Baltics first — Estonia, Lithuania and Latvia all already above 7 %.

Oct 2022 · Peak

The map turns red

The October 2022 reading — 11.5 % for the euro area, 17.4 % for the Baltics on average — was the highest in the euro's existence. By this point hardly any country sits below five percent.

2024 · Cooling

Most of the heat drains away

By late 2024 the map has cooled back into greens and yellows, but unevenly. Hover any country for its 2019-to-now mini-trend — some never overshot much, some have not yet returned.

Explore

Try the slider

Drag the timeline to watch the map breathe in and out of crisis. Pause on any month. The Baltic spike is unmistakable; so is the slow Spanish climb that peaks half a year later.

Categories

It was not just one thing

HICP — the basket Eurostat uses — splits into a dozen broad categories: food, housing, energy, transport, services. Each panel shows one. Most lines stay near zero through 2020. Then they don't.

Energy first

The energy panel lights up first

Look at Housing, water, electricity. It peels off in mid-2021, six months ahead of the other categories. By March 2022 it is well above 30 %. This is the lit fuse on the rest of the chart.

Food follows

Food picks up the wave

Food prices follow energy with about a six-month lag — fertiliser, transport and processing costs catching up. The food panel peaks above 15 % in early 2023, after energy has already begun to fall.

Services last

Services rise quietly and stay

The services line never spikes the way energy does, but it climbs steadily and refuses to come back down — the textbook signature of inflation moving from imported costs into domestic wages and rents.

Act II · 2020 – 2021

The Shock

A pandemic shuts down half the global economy; then re-opens it all at once. Container ships queue off the coast of Los Angeles. Furniture costs double. Used cars become collateral. The signal of the crisis is not yet visible to most consumers — but it is loud on the chart.

The single line

EU-27 inflation, one number a month

Stripped to a single line, the story is almost cleaner than it should be. The flat decade of 2009–2020. The COVID dip. The reopening climb. The October 2022 peak. The slow, uneven descent.

March 2020

The pandemic dent

For a brief moment in spring 2020, deflation looked like the bigger risk. Energy prices collapsed; oil briefly traded negative; demand for everything except groceries and games consoles fell off a cliff.

Feb 2022

Ukraine, and the gas valves

Russia's invasion of Ukraine on 24 February 2022 turned an already-rising line vertical. The orange band marks the gas-price spike that followed; everything indexed to wholesale gas — heating, electricity, fertiliser, food — followed it up.

Jul 2022 – Sep 2023

The ECB hike cycle

The ECB raised its deposit rate ten times in fourteen months, from –0.5 % to 4.0 %. The line peaks, then falls — though not all at the same speed.

Spread, not average

The average hides the spread

Every line you have seen so far averages the EU. This chart fans the distribution open. Each ridge is one year. The width of the ridge shows how spread out the 27 country-level rates were.

2019

A narrow, tidy ridge

In 2019 nearly every country sat between 0 and 2 percent. The 2019 ridge is sharp and narrow — Europe was, by this measure, a uniform inflation environment.

2022

The ridge bursts

The 2022 ridge is squashed and stretched across half the chart. Estonia sat at 19.4 % while France held under 6 %. The same currency union, fifteen points apart.

2024

It narrows again — mostly

By 2024 the ridge has tightened, but it has not snapped back to its old shape. The Baltic tail is shorter; the centre still runs hotter than 2019.

Act III · 2022 – 2023

The Squeeze

Energy markets break. Bills double, then triple. Governments scramble cap-and-rebate schemes. Then the wave moves through food, then services, then services-after-food, until everyone — not just the heating bill — has noticed.

Decomposed

Three drivers, one number

Eurostat's headline rate is a weighted average of dozens of sub-indices. Stack the three largest — energy, food, services — and you can see which is doing the work in any given month.

Energy dominates

The orange wall

From late 2021 through 2022, energy alone accounts for more than half of the headline figure. The orange band swells until it is almost the whole chart.

Hand-off

Food takes over

By spring 2023 energy is contributing nothing; it is the green band — food — that holds the headline above 7 %. This is the textbook second-round effect: yesterday's energy bill becomes today's fertiliser cost.

Stubborn services

Why the ECB kept hiking

Even in 2024, the purple slice — services — refuses to drop below the ECB's target. Wages chasing prices, rents catching up to leases signed in cheaper times, restaurants passing through last year's energy bill.

Two points

What an electricity bill cost · then and now

Eurostat publishes household electricity prices twice a year. We plot two: H1 2019 and H1 2024. Each line is one country. The slope tells you the story without much else needed.

Steepest

The Czech and Romanian climbs

Czechia roughly doubled. So did Romania. Both started from low bases and rely heavily on natural-gas-fired generation; both went up the most in percentage terms.

Quiet

Spain and Portugal — almost flat

The Iberian exception: an EU-approved cap on gas prices fed into electricity from mid-2022. The slope is barely positive. Whether this was good policy or expensive subsidy is a question for a different essay.

Twenty-seven rows. Twelve columns. Each cell is the average inflation a country saw in a given category over a year. Reds are bad, greens are calm.

Act IV · 2023 – 2025

The Aftermath

Headline inflation is back near target. But what is left behind is a price level that does not return — and a question about whether wages caught up enough to live with it.

Real wage change

Did pay keep up?

Real wage change is nominal wage growth minus inflation. We compute it for every EU country with a statutory minimum wage, 2019 → 2024. Bars going right mean wages outran prices. Bars going left mean prices outran wages.

Winners

The east caught up

Romania, Bulgaria, Lithuania, Hungary — every single country that started 2019 with a minimum wage under €500 saw it climb in real terms. Catch-up convergence didn't pause for the crisis.

Losers

The west fell behind

Czechia, Estonia, Belgium — all bars going left. Real purchasing power on a minimum-wage paycheck shrank between five and twelve percent.

What €100 buys you today

Pick a country

Pick your country

The waffle redraws. In Germany, your €100 is worth about €81. In Estonia, about €61. The squares that fade out are the purchasing power you lost.

Pick a category

Pick what you were buying

For energy, that €100 buys roughly half as much as it did in 2019. For services, around 80 cents on the euro. For food, around 70.

Two axes

Wages on one axis, prices on the other

The horizontal axis is cumulative HICP since 2019. The vertical axis is cumulative nominal-wage growth. The diagonal is the break-even line where they tie. Points above the diagonal: pay won. Below it: prices won.

A path per country

One country, one path

Each path traces a country from 2019 to 2024. The shape of the path matters: a steep climb early, a flat tail, suggests pay caught up first then lagged. A flat early-section then a recent vertical rise suggests pay finally responded.

Hover one

Act V · 2025

Five Years Later

The crisis is over, by the textbook definition. But its fingerprint is on the price level, the wage league, and the political vocabulary of the continent. Two final charts to close the file.

Rankings

Who was the most expensive country?

Each year we rank the 27 EU countries from most-expensive to least-expensive household electricity. The bump chart shows how those rankings shuffled across the crisis.

Movers

The Czech leap

Czechia jumps from rank 19 in 2019 to rank 6 in 2024 — bigger relative climb than any other country.

Anchors

Some lines stay almost flat

France barely moves. Germany barely moves. Bulgaria barely moves. The crisis re-ranked the middle of the league more than the ends.

Spread

The cheap-to-dear gap, by year

One box per year. The box is the inter-quartile range of country annual inflation rates. The line in the middle is the median. The dots are outliers.

2022

The widest box

The 2022 box is the tallest in the series. The whiskers reach to +19.4 % at the top, +5.9 % at the bottom — a gap of nearly fourteen percentage points within a single currency union.

2024

The narrowest in three years

The 2024 box is the tightest since 2020 — but its median sits above 2019's, and one dot pokes out from the top. The gap is closing; it has not closed.

How this was made

All data are pulled directly from the Eurostat JSON API by the Python scripts in preprocessing/. Six tables: harmonised consumer prices (monthly, annual, indexed), household electricity prices, minimum wages, and the house price index. A topojson of EU borders comes from the leakymirror fork of world-atlas. No proprietary sources.

The site is hand-written HTML, CSS custom properties, and ES modules. The only run-time dependencies are D3 v7, Scrollama, topojson-client and d3-annotation — all loaded as globals from a CDN. Animations use the lightweight Motion One library plus CSS transitions, with a static fallback wherever prefers-reduced-motion is set.

The full rationale for every chart — what question it answers, what alternatives were rejected, what depth criteria it passes — is in docs/chart_rationale/. Source code is at github.com/<handle>/price-of-europe.
