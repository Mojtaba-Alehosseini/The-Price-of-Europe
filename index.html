The European Central Bank aims for two percent inflation a year. For most of the 2010s that target held — then, from 2021, prices across Europe climbed faster than at any time in the euro's history, peaking near eleven percent. This essay traces where that shock landed, what drove it, and whether wages kept up. All figures are Eurostat.

Act I · 2015 – 2019

The Calm

Five quiet years when the European Central Bank worried, mostly, about prices being too low. The geography of inflation looked nearly uniform — Estonia, Italy, Portugal all roughly the same shade of nothing-to-see.

2019 · Before

A continent on cruise control

In 2019 the EU's annual inflation rate was 1.4 % — comfortably below the ECB's target. The map sits almost entirely in the muted greens of the sub-target band.

2021 · Heating

The first shade darker

As supply chains unkinked after the pandemic, prices began catching up. By December 2021 the eurozone hit 5.0 %. The map turns amber across the Baltics first — Estonia, Lithuania and Latvia all already above 7 %.

Oct 2022 · Peak

The map turns red

The October 2022 peak — 11.5 % across the EU-27, 17 %+ across the Baltics — was the highest reading since the euro began. The annual average for 2022 still settled at 9.2 %, but hardly any country closed the year below five percent.

2024 · Cooling

Most of the heat drains away

By late 2024 the map has cooled back into greens and yellows, but unevenly. Click any country to see its 2015-to-now annual trajectory — some never overshot much, some have not yet returned.

Explore

Try the slider

Drag the timeline to watch the map breathe in and out of crisis. Pause on any month. The Baltic spike is unmistakable; so is the slow Spanish climb that peaks half a year later.

From geography to ranking

Line up every country

The map showed where the shock landed. Now flatten it — every country lined up against the EU-27 average, ranked by how far prices have run since January 2019.

Five years, traced

The whole story, one country at a time

Under each bar an 84-month sparkline traces 2019 → today. Faint ticks mark Russia's invasion, the ECB's first hike, the peak rate, and the first cut. Five years compressed into a single frame.

Categories

It was not just one thing

HICP — the basket Eurostat uses — splits into a dozen broad categories: food, housing, energy, transport, services. Each panel shows one. Most lines stay near zero through 2020. Then they don't.

Energy first

The energy panel lights up first

The electricity, gas & fuels panel is the fuse. It crosses 5 % inflation in April 2021 — seven months before the headline rate does — and runs past 50 % by late 2022. Every other category only lights after it.

Food follows

Food picks up the wave

Food picks up the wave the better part of a year later: it does not cross 5 % until February 2022, ten months after energy. Fertiliser, transport and processing costs catch up, and the food panel peaks near 19 % in spring 2023 — after energy has already begun to fall.

Services last

Services rise quietly and stay

The services line never spikes the way energy does, but it climbs steadily and refuses to come back down — the textbook signature of inflation moving from imported costs into domestic wages and rents.

Act II · 2020 – 2021

The Shock

A pandemic shuts down half the global economy; then re-opens it all at once. Container ships queue off the coast of Los Angeles. Furniture costs double. Used cars become collateral. The signal of the crisis is not yet visible to most consumers — but it is loud on the chart.

The single line

EU-27 inflation, one number a month

Stripped to a single line, the story is almost cleaner than it should be. The flat decade of 2009–2020. The COVID dip. The reopening climb. The October 2022 peak. The slow, uneven descent.

March 2020

The pandemic dent

For a brief moment in spring 2020, deflation looked like the bigger risk. Energy prices collapsed; oil briefly traded negative; demand for everything except groceries and games consoles fell off a cliff.

Feb 2022

Ukraine, and the gas valves

Russia's invasion of Ukraine on 24 February 2022 turned an already-rising line vertical. The orange band marks the gas-price spike that followed; everything indexed to wholesale gas — heating, electricity, fertiliser, food — followed it up.

Jul 2022 – Sep 2023

The ECB hike cycle

The ECB raised its deposit rate ten times in fourteen months, from –0.5 % to 4.0 %. The line peaks, then falls — though not all at the same speed.

Spread, not average

The average hides the spread

Every line you have seen so far averages the EU. This chart fans the distribution open. Each ridge is one year. The width of the ridge shows how spread out the 27 country-level rates were.

2019

A narrow, tidy ridge

In 2019 nearly every country sat between 0 and 2 percent. The 2019 ridge is sharp and narrow — Europe was, by this measure, a uniform inflation environment.

2022

The ridge bursts

The 2022 ridge is squashed and stretched across half the chart. Estonia sat at 19.4 % while France held under 6 %. The same currency union, fifteen points apart.

2024

It narrows again — mostly

By 2024 the ridge has tightened, but it has not snapped back to its old shape. The Baltic tail is shorter; the centre still runs hotter than 2019.

Act III · 2022 – 2023

The Squeeze

Energy markets break. Bills double, then triple. Governments scramble cap-and-rebate schemes. Then the wave moves through food, then services, then services-after-food, until everyone — not just the heating bill — has noticed.

Decomposed

Three drivers, one number

Eurostat's headline rate is a weighted average of dozens of sub-indices. Stack the three largest — energy, food, services — and you can see which is doing the work in any given month.

Energy dominates

The orange wall

From late 2021 through 2022, energy alone accounts for more than half of the headline figure. The orange band swells until it is almost the whole chart. Behind that EU average the shock diverged sharply by grid: Lithuania's household electricity price nearly doubled between 2019 and 2024, and Estonia's and Czechia's climbed around three-quarters — yet Spain and Portugal, shielded by an EU-approved gas-price cap, were barely touched.

Hand-off

Food takes over

By spring 2023 energy is contributing nothing; it is the green band — food — that holds the headline above 7 %. This is the textbook second-round effect: yesterday's energy bill becomes today's fertiliser cost.

Stubborn services

Why the ECB kept hiking

Even in 2024, the purple slice — services — refuses to drop below the ECB's target. Wages chasing prices, rents catching up to leases signed in cheaper times, restaurants passing through last year's energy bill.

The whole field

Twenty-seven countries, seven years

Every EU country down the side, every year from 2019 to 2025 across the top. Each cell is that country's headline inflation that year — greens are calm, reds are bad, the darkest wine cells are the worst. Rows are sorted by 2022, so the hardest-hit float to the top.

2022

One column lights up

Run your eye down the 2022 column. There is no green in it. Every one of the twenty-seven ran hot — and even the coolest, France, sat near 6 %, three times the ECB's target.

The Baltics

Three rows burn hottest

At the very top sit Estonia, Lithuania and Latvia — small, gas-reliant economies whose 2022 cells run past 17 %. The same shock, but the heat was not shared evenly.

The peak

One cell, the worst of all

The single hottest cell in the whole essay: Estonia in 2022, at 19.4 %. Hover any cell to read its exact figure.

Act IV · 2023 – 2025

The Aftermath

Headline inflation is back near target. But what is left behind is a price level that does not return — and a question about whether wages caught up enough to live with it.

Real wage change

Did pay keep up?

Real wage change deflates each minimum wage by its own inflation: (1 + nominal wage growth) / (1 + HICP growth) − 1. We compute it for every EU country with a statutory minimum wage, 2019 → 2024. Bars going right mean wages outran prices; bars going left mean prices outran wages.

Winners

The south and the Balkans pulled ahead

Croatia, Portugal, Greece, Latvia, Bulgaria, Spain — fifteen of the twenty-one countries raised their minimum wage faster than prices rose. Croatia leads: a minimum-wage paycheck bought about 23 % more in real terms in 2024 than in 2019.

Losers

Where inflation won

Czechia, Hungary, Estonia, Romania — six countries let prices outrun pay. Real purchasing power on a minimum-wage paycheck shrank by up to thirteen percent in Czechia, despite double-digit nominal raises, because inflation climbed even faster.

€100 then · €? now

What €100 buys you today

Take €100 from January 2019. Each square is one of those euros. Watch them erode: across the EU-27, the same money now stretches to only about €77 of goods — twenty-three euros, quietly gone.

Pick a country

Where the loss bites hardest

The waffle redraws by country. In Germany, your €100 still buys about €78. In Estonia, about €66. In Hungary, just €61 — the hatched squares are purchasing power that no longer exists.

Pick a category

And on what you were buying

Now hold the country at the EU-27 and switch the basket. Services held up best at about €79; food fell to €71; electricity & gas took the hardest hit — that €100 now buys only about €66 of it.

Act V · 2025

Five Years Later

The crisis is over, by the textbook definition. But its fingerprint is on the price level, the wage league, and the political vocabulary of the continent. Two final charts to close the file.

Two axes

Wages on one axis, prices on the other

The horizontal axis is cumulative HICP since 2019. The vertical axis is cumulative nominal-wage growth. The diagonal is the break-even line where they tie. Points above the diagonal: pay won. Below it: prices won.

Estonia

Pay spent three years chasing prices

Estonia traces the loss in one line. Prices ran +41 %; the minimum wage only stirred in 2024, ending +29 % — twelve points short. Its path never crosses back above the break-even diagonal.

Bulgaria

Where pay won

Not everyone lost. Bulgaria's minimum wage rose +50 % against +32 % prices — eighteen points ahead, above the line the whole way. Tap or hover any path to read its own race.

Who lost most

Czechia — and the electricity league

Czechia is the widest miss of all: pay +23 % against prices +41 %. It is also where the energy shock bit hardest — Czech household electricity climbed from mid-pack to among the EU's dearest grids, the sharpest rise up the league.

Spread

The cheap-to-dear gap, by year

One box per year. The box is the inter-quartile range of country annual inflation rates. The line in the middle is the median. The dots are outliers.

2022

The widest box

The 2022 box is the tallest in the series. The whiskers reach to +19.4 % at the top, +5.9 % at the bottom — a gap of nearly fourteen percentage points within a single currency union.

2024

The narrowest in three years

The 2024 box is the tightest since 2020 — but its median sits above 2019's, and one dot pokes out from the top. The gap is closing; it has not closed.

How this was made

All data are pulled directly from the Eurostat JSON API by the Python scripts in preprocessing/. Six tables: harmonised consumer prices (monthly, annual, indexed), household electricity prices, minimum wages, and the house price index. A topojson of EU borders comes from the leakymirror fork of world-atlas. No proprietary sources.

The site is hand-written HTML, CSS custom properties, and ES modules. The only run-time dependencies are D3 v7, Scrollama, topojson-client, d3-annotation, and flubber (used in the Choropleth → Bar morph for path interpolation) — all loaded as globals from a CDN. Animations use the project's own lightweight MotionManager module plus D3 transitions and CSS keyframes, with a static fallback wherever prefers-reduced-motion is set.

Methodological choices worth knowing

  • EU-27 aggregate — Choropleth uses Eurostat's official EU27_2020 annual HICP figures (1.4 % for 2019, 9.2 % for 2022, 2.6 % for 2024) rather than a naïve country-mean.
  • Small-multiples peaks — each per-category peak label marks the global month-with-maximum-YoY rate across the visible 2010 – 2025 window, not a windowed local maximum.
  • Heatmap aggregation — cells show the mean of the 12 monthly YoY rates for each (country, category) in the selected year, not Eurostat's pre-published annual rate. The two differ by a fraction of a percent in most cases.
  • Stacked-area weighting — the four bands use illustrative proxy weights (energy × 0.30, food × 0.20, services × 0.40, residual = other), not Eurostat's official HICP contribution decomposition. Treat shapes-over-time as the truth; treat absolute levels as illustrative.
  • Real-wage formula — Diverging-bar and Connected-scatter compute real change as (1 + nominal wage Δ) / (1 + cumulative HICP Δ) − 1. Six EU countries are excluded from these charts: Austria, Cyprus, Denmark, Finland, Italy, Sweden — they set wages through collective bargaining, not a statutory minimum.
  • Ridgeline KDE — Epanechnikov kernel, bandwidth = 1.6, sampled at 0.4 pp intervals across the rate domain.
  • Box-plot outliers — Tukey's 1.5 × IQR rule, applied per year across the 27 country values. Outliers shown as larger red dots; the faint dot strip beneath each box is every country's actual value with deterministic horizontal jitter.

The full rationale for every chart — what question it answers, what alternatives were rejected, what depth criteria it passes — is in docs/chart_rationale/. Source code is at github.com/mojtaba-alehoseini/price-of-europe.
